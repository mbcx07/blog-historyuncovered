The Cuban Missile Crisis: The Submarine That Almost Fired a Nuclear Torpedo

2026-07-31 · Modern History · Tags: modern history, The

October 1962. The world held its breath as the United States and the Soviet Union stood on the precipice of nuclear annihilation. For thirteen days, the Cuban Missile Crisis dominated headlines, terrified citizens, and tested the nerves of leaders in Washington and Moscow. We all know the broad strokes: American U-2 spy planes discovered Soviet nuclear missiles in Cuba, President John F. Kennedy imposed a naval blockade, and after tense negotiations, the missiles were removed. But beneath this familiar narrative lies a deeper, more harrowing story—one of miscommunication, human error, and a single Soviet submarine commander who came within seconds of launching a nuclear torpedo that would have triggered World War III. This is the untold story of the men who stared into the abyss and somehow pulled back.

The Discovery That Shook the World

On October 14, 1962, an American U-2 spy plane piloted by Major Richard Heyser captured photographs of Soviet medium-range ballistic missiles being assembled in San Cristóbal, Cuba. The images were unmistakable: nuclear warheads capable of striking Washington, D.C., and nearly every major U.S. city within minutes. President Kennedy was briefed the next morning, and the gravity of the situation hit him like a freight train. The Soviet Union had promised they would never place offensive weapons in Cuba—this was a direct lie.

Kennedy convened a secret group of advisors, later known as EXCOMM (Executive Committee of the National Security Council). For days, they debated options: airstrikes, invasion, or a naval blockade. The military brass, led by General Curtis LeMay, pushed for immediate invasion. LeMay famously told Kennedy, "They're in your gut, Mr. President. You've got to do something." But Kennedy, haunted by the memory of the Bay of Pigs fiasco, chose a more measured approach: a quarantine of Cuba to prevent further Soviet shipments. On October 22, he addressed the nation, revealing the missiles and announcing the blockade. The world braced for war.

The Soviet Gamble: Why Khrushchev Risked It All

To understand the crisis, we must step into Nikita Khrushchev's shoes. The Soviet premier was acutely aware of the strategic imbalance. The United States had nuclear missiles in Turkey, Italy, and West Germany—all within striking distance of the Soviet Union. Meanwhile, Soviet intercontinental ballistic missiles (ICBMs) were few and unreliable. Khrushchev saw an opportunity: by secretly placing missiles in Cuba, he could level the playing field, protect his ally Fidel Castro, and gain a powerful bargaining chip. It was a bold, reckless gamble.

The deployment was carried out in utmost secrecy. Soviet ships transported missiles, launchers, and thousands of troops to Cuba, all under the cover of darkness. But the Americans were watching. By mid-October, U-2 flights had detected the telltale signs of missile sites. Khrushchev had underestimated Kennedy's resolve and the effectiveness of American intelligence. When the quarantine was announced, Soviet ships approaching Cuba were ordered to turn back—except for a few that were allowed through after inspection. The tension escalated daily, with both sides engaging in a dangerous game of brinkmanship.

The Submarine That Almost Ended the World

While the world focused on the surface ships and the missile sites, a far more dangerous drama was unfolding beneath the waves. Four Soviet Foxtrot-class submarines—B-4, B-36, B-59, and B-130—had been dispatched to Cuba as part of Operation Kama. Each carried a nuclear-tipped torpedo, with a yield of 15 kilotons—roughly the size of the bomb dropped on Hiroshima. Their mission: to protect the Soviet ships and, if necessary, defend Cuba against an American invasion.

On October 27, the USS Beale, a U.S. Navy destroyer, detected the B-59 near the quarantine line. The Americans began dropping practice depth charges—small explosives designed to force the submarine to surface. Inside the B-59, the crew had been submerged for days, with little communication and rising temperatures. The depth charges were deafening, and the crew believed they were under attack. The captain, Valentin Savitsky, was exhausted and furious. He ordered the nuclear torpedo to be prepared for launch. According to the rules of engagement, the submarine could fire the torpedo only with the unanimous consent of three senior officers: the captain, the political officer, and the fleet commander.

Savitsky was ready to fire. The political officer, Ivan Maslennikov, also agreed. But the third man, Vasili Arkhipov, the fleet commander, hesitated. Arkhipov was a seasoned officer who had survived a nuclear accident on a submarine in 1961. He knew the horrors of radiation and the consequences of a nuclear exchange. He argued that the depth charges were not necessarily an attack—they were a signal to surface. He refused to give his consent. For what seemed like an eternity, the crew argued, sweat dripping down their faces. Finally, Savitsky relented, and the submarine surfaced. The world had come within a hair's breadth of nuclear war—not from a missile silo, but from a single man's refusal to launch.

The Backchannel: Secret Letters and a Deal That Saved the World

While the submarine crisis unfolded, Kennedy and Khrushchev were engaged in a frantic exchange of letters. The public messages were formal and confrontational, but behind the scenes, a secret backchannel was opened. Robert Kennedy, the Attorney General, met with Soviet Ambassador Anatoly Dobrynin in a clandestine meeting at the Justice Department. The message was clear: the United States would remove its Jupiter missiles from Turkey, but only if the Soviet Union removed its missiles from Cuba—and the deal had to remain secret to save face for both leaders.

Khrushchev, desperate to avoid a nuclear war, accepted. On October 28, he announced that the Soviet Union would dismantle the missiles in Cuba in exchange for a public pledge from the United States not to invade the island. The secret part—the removal of the Turkish missiles—was kept quiet for months. The crisis was over, but the world had learned a terrifying lesson. The two superpowers had come closer to mutual destruction than anyone had realized. In the aftermath, a direct hotline was established between Washington and Moscow to prevent future miscommunications. The Partial Test Ban Treaty followed in 1963, and the arms race, while still intense, began to show signs of restraint.

The Legacy: Lessons from the Brink

The Cuban Missile Crisis remains the closest the world has ever come to nuclear war. It is a testament to the fragility of human decision-making under extreme pressure. The story of Vasili Arkhipov is a powerful reminder that individual courage can alter the course of history. Had he not refused to launch, the first nuclear exchange might have occurred—and the world as we know it would not exist. Yet, the crisis also exposed the dangers of secrecy, miscommunication, and the assumption that the enemy is rational. Both Kennedy and Khrushchev made mistakes, but they also showed a willingness to step back from the brink.

Today, as we face new global challenges—from cyberwarfare to climate change—the lessons of October 1962 are more relevant than ever. The crisis teaches us that diplomacy, even in the darkest hours, can prevail. It reminds us that leaders must be willing to listen, to compromise, and to recognize the humanity in their adversaries. And it honors the quiet heroes like Arkhipov, who, in a moment of chaos, chose reason over rage.

Conclusion

The Cuban Missile Crisis was not just a political standoff; it was a human drama played out on a global stage. From the U-2 pilots who risked their lives to the sailors trapped in sweltering submarines, from the exhausted presidents to the anonymous officers who made split-second decisions, the crisis was a mosaic of courage, fear, and fallibility. We often remember the big names—Kennedy, Khrushchev, Castro—but we must also remember the unsung individuals who, through their actions or inactions, saved us all. As we look back on those thirteen days, we are reminded that history is not inevitable. It is shaped by choices, and the most important choices are often made in the shadows, far from the cameras and the headlines. The submarine that almost fired a nuclear torpedo is a haunting symbol of how close we came—and a powerful argument for why we must never forget.
