The Pig War: The Bloodless Conflict That Nearly Started a War Between the US and Britain

2026-07-23 · Wars · Tags: The, wars, historyuncovered

In the annals of military history, wars are typically remembered for their carnage, their strategic brilliance, or their lasting geopolitical consequences. But every so often, a conflict emerges that defies all expectations—a war that is fought not with cannons and muskets, but with stubbornness, diplomacy, and a single, unfortunate pig. The Pig War of 1859, a standoff between the United States and Great Britain over a tiny island in the Pacific Northwest, is one such anomaly. It is a story of territorial ambition, national pride, and a pig that nearly ignited a third Anglo-American war. Yet, in the end, it became a testament to the power of restraint and the absurdity of human conflict. This is the tale of how a stray porker almost changed the course of North American history—and why it remains one of the most fascinating military standoffs ever recorded.

The Disputed Ground: San Juan Island and the Oregon Treaty

To understand the Pig War, one must first understand the geography and the diplomatic mess that created it. The Oregon Treaty of 1846 had settled the long-standing boundary dispute between the United States and British North America (modern-day Canada) by drawing the 49th parallel as the border from the Rocky Mountains to the Pacific Ocean. However, the treaty contained a critical ambiguity: it stated that the boundary would follow the 49th parallel to the middle of the Strait of Georgia, then turn south through the Haro Strait or the Rosario Strait—whichever was the main channel—to the Strait of Juan de Fuca. The problem? The treaty did not specify which strait was the main one, and the San Juan Islands, a cluster of over 170 islands, sat squarely in the middle of this ambiguity.

Both nations claimed the islands, particularly the largest, San Juan Island, which offered strategic access to the Strait of Georgia and the Fraser River, a vital fur-trade route. The Hudson's Bay Company, a British fur-trading giant, had established a salmon-curing station on San Juan Island in the 1840s, while American settlers began arriving in the 1850s, drawn by the promise of fertile land and the potential for a deep-water port. By 1859, the island was a patchwork of British and American claims, with each side asserting its sovereignty through settlement and occasional shows of force. The stage was set for a spark—and that spark came in the form of a black boar.

The Incident: A Pig, a Farmer, and a Trigger

On June 15, 1859, an American farmer named Lyman Cutlar discovered a large black pig rooting through his potato patch on the southern end of San Juan Island. Cutlar, who had recently planted the potatoes, was furious. The pig, he knew, belonged to Charles Griffin, an Irishman who managed the Hudson's Bay Company's sheep farm nearby. The two men had already clashed over the pig's wandering habits, and Cutlar had warned Griffin to keep the animal penned. But on that fateful morning, the pig was again in his garden, and Cutlar, in a fit of rage, shot and killed it.

When Griffin learned of the pig's death, he demanded compensation—$100, a sum that Cutlar considered exorbitant. Cutlar offered to pay $10, but Griffin refused, and the dispute escalated. Cutlar, fearing arrest by British authorities, appealed to the American military for protection. Meanwhile, Griffin reported the incident to the British authorities in Victoria, who threatened to arrest Cutlar. The situation might have remained a local squabble, but it quickly spiraled into an international crisis. The American military, under the command of Brigadier General William S. Harney, saw an opportunity to assert American claims to the island. Harney, a fiery and expansionist officer, dispatched a company of 66 soldiers under Captain George E. Pickett (who would later gain infamy at Gettysburg) to San Juan Island with orders to protect American settlers and prevent British interference.

The Standoff: Two Navies, One Island, and a Lot of Bluffing

When Pickett and his men landed on July 27, 1859, they found a tense but peaceful situation. The British, however, were not about to back down. Rear Admiral Robert L. Baynes, commander of the British Pacific fleet, received word of the American landing and dispatched three warships—the HMS Tribune, the HMS Satellite, and the HMS Plumper—to San Juan Island. The British ships anchored in the harbor, their guns trained on the American camp. The Americans, in turn, fortified their position with earthworks and artillery. By August, over 400 American soldiers and 2,000 British troops (including marines and sailors) faced off across the island, with cannons aimed at each other. The situation was a powder keg, and the only thing preventing an explosion was the restraint of the local commanders.

Admiral Baynes, a veteran of the Napoleonic Wars, was reportedly horrified by the prospect of war over a pig. He famously remarked, "It would be a great disgrace to two great nations to go to war about a pig." He refused to order an attack, despite pressure from the British governor, James Douglas, who wanted to expel the Americans. On the American side, Captain Pickett was equally cautious, though he was known for his bravado. The two sides settled into a tense standoff, with soldiers from both nations fraternizing, trading goods, and even sharing meals while their officers negotiated. The standoff lasted for months, with no shots fired—only the occasional pig wandering between the lines.

The Resolution: Diplomacy Over Gunpowder

News of the standoff eventually reached Washington, D.C., and London. Both governments, still recovering from the recent Crimean War and wary of a costly conflict, were eager to avoid war. President James Buchanan and British Prime Minister Lord Palmerston agreed to a peaceful resolution. In September 1859, they dispatched a joint commission to negotiate a temporary settlement. The commission agreed to a joint military occupation of San Juan Island, with each side maintaining a small garrison—no more than 100 men—while the boundary dispute was referred to arbitration. The American troops were placed under the command of Captain Lewis C. Hunt, and the British under Captain George Bazalgette. The two garrisons coexisted peacefully for the next 12 years, with soldiers from both nations even celebrating holidays together.

The final resolution came in 1871, when the Treaty of Washington established a commission to settle the boundary. The commission, led by Kaiser Wilhelm I of Germany, was tasked with deciding whether the Haro Strait or the Rosario Strait was the main channel. After reviewing the evidence, the Kaiser ruled in favor of the United States, citing the deeper and wider Haro Strait as the primary navigational route. On October 21, 1872, the San Juan Islands were officially awarded to the United States. The British garrison withdrew, and the American flag was raised over the island. The Pig War had ended without a single casualty—except, of course, for the pig.

Legacy and Lessons: The Absurdity and Wisdom of Restraint

The Pig War is often cited as a prime example of how diplomacy and common sense can prevent catastrophic conflict. It is a reminder that even the most trivial incidents can escalate into war if leaders are reckless, but also that wise leadership can defuse tensions. The standoff on San Juan Island demonstrated the importance of clear communication, mutual respect, and the willingness to seek arbitration rather than resort to force. It also highlighted the role of local commanders in preventing escalation—Admiral Baynes and Captain Pickett, despite their orders, chose to prioritize peace over pride.

Today, San Juan Island is a popular tourist destination, and the site of the standoff is preserved as the San Juan Island National Historical Park. Visitors can explore the American Camp and the British Camp, where the two garrisons once faced each other. The park serves as a living monument to the absurdity of war and the power of peaceful resolution. The pig, ironically, has become a symbol of the conflict—a reminder that sometimes the smallest things can have the largest consequences. As we reflect on the Pig War, we are reminded that history is not always about grand battles and heroic sacrifices; sometimes, it is about a farmer, a pig, and the wisdom to know when not to fight.

Conclusion

The Pig War stands as a unique chapter in the history of international relations—a conflict that was fought with words, not weapons, and resolved through arbitration rather than bloodshed. It is a story that challenges our assumptions about war and peace, showing that even in the midst of territorial disputes and national pride, there is room for restraint and reason. The pig that started it all may have been a simple farm animal, but its legacy is a testament to the human capacity for both folly and wisdom. As we study the Pig War, we are reminded that the greatest victories are often those that never happen—and that sometimes, the best way to win a war is to refuse to fight it.
