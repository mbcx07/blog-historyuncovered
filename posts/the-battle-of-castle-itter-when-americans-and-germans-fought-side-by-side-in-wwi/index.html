The Battle of Castle Itter: When Americans and Germans Fought Side by Side in WWII

2026-07-08 · Wars · Tags: The, wars

In the final, chaotic days of World War II, as the Third Reich crumbled and the Allies closed in from all sides, an extraordinary event unfolded in the Austrian Alps—a battle that defied every convention of the conflict. It was a fight where American soldiers, German Wehrmacht troops, and Austrian resistance fighters stood shoulder to shoulder against the SS, all to defend a medieval castle and the high-profile French prisoners held within. This was the Battle of Castle Itter, a skirmish so improbable that it has been called the strangest battle of the entire war. It is a story of unlikely alliances, desperate courage, and the human instinct to choose honor over ideology, even in the face of certain death.

The Castle and Its Prisoners

Perched on a hill overlooking the village of Itter in the Tyrolean Alps, Schloss Itter was a 19th-century castle that had been converted into a prison in 1943. The Nazis used it to hold high-value French prisoners—men and women whose influence and status made them valuable bargaining chips in the final months of the war. Among them were former French prime ministers Édouard Daladier and Paul Reynaud, two generals who had commanded French forces in 1940, and the famous tennis star and resistance fighter Jean Borotra. Also imprisoned were Marie-Agnès de Gaulle, the sister of General Charles de Gaulle, and the controversial politician André François-Poncet. These were not ordinary inmates; they were the living symbols of a France that had been humiliated and divided, and the SS intended to use them as hostages in a last-ditch stand.

As April 1945 turned to May, the situation became desperate. The SS guards at Itter were under orders to execute the prisoners if the Allies approached. But the commandant, a man named Sebastian Wimmer, was more pragmatic than fanatical. He knew the war was lost, and he had no desire to die for a cause that was already dead. When the prisoners learned of the approaching American forces, they began to plan their own liberation. They had a secret ally inside the castle: a Czech cook named Andreas Krobot, who had been smuggling messages to the outside world. Through him, they made contact with the local Austrian resistance, who in turn reached out to the advancing American 12th Armored Division.

The German Officer Who Switched Sides

One of the most remarkable figures in this story is Major Josef Gangl, a German Wehrmacht officer who had become disillusioned with the Nazi regime. Gangl was a decorated veteran of the Eastern Front, but by 1945 he had seen enough of the horrors of war. He had joined a group of Austrian resistance fighters in the town of Wörgl, and when he learned of the prisoners at Itter, he decided to act. Gangl knew that the SS were planning to execute the prisoners, and he was determined to prevent it. He contacted the Americans, who were advancing from the west, and offered his assistance. The American commander, Captain John C. "Jack" Lee Jr., was initially skeptical, but he quickly realized that Gangl's knowledge of the local terrain and the German military could be invaluable.

Lee and Gangl formed an unlikely partnership. Lee, a tough, no-nonsense officer from the 23rd Tank Battalion, had a small force of about a dozen men and a single Sherman tank named "Besotten Jenny." Gangl, meanwhile, had a handful of German soldiers who had also turned against the SS, along with a few Austrian resistance fighters. Together, they hatched a plan to reach the castle and evacuate the prisoners before the SS could carry out their orders. It was a mission that seemed doomed from the start—they were vastly outnumbered, and the SS forces in the area were still formidable. But they pressed on, driven by a shared conviction that the prisoners' lives were worth more than any allegiance to a dying regime.

The Race to the Castle

On the morning of May 4, 1945, Lee and his men, along with Gangl and his small band, set out for Castle Itter. The journey was treacherous, as the roads were clogged with retreating German troops and the threat of ambush was constant. When they finally arrived, they found the castle in a state of near-panic. The SS guards had fled, but the prisoners were still inside, and they were terrified that the SS would return with reinforcements. Lee and Gangl quickly assessed the situation. They decided to fortify the castle and prepare for a siege, rather than attempt a risky evacuation. The prisoners, many of whom were elderly or in poor health, would be safer behind the castle's thick walls than on the open road.

The defenders consisted of Lee's small American force, Gangl's German defectors, a few Austrian resistance fighters, and even some of the prisoners themselves. Jean Borotra, the tennis champion, proved to be a surprisingly capable marksman, and he volunteered to take up a rifle. The group also had the Sherman tank, which they positioned at the main gate as a mobile artillery piece. They had no radio contact with the outside world, so they were completely cut off. Their only hope was that reinforcements would arrive before the SS did.

The Battle Itself

On the morning of May 5, the SS attacked. A force of about 150 Waffen-SS soldiers, led by the fanatical SS-Obersturmführer Georg Bochmann, descended on the castle. They had been ordered to recapture the prisoners and execute them, and they were determined to do so. The defenders, numbering fewer than 30, were vastly outnumbered. The battle raged for hours, with the SS launching wave after wave of attacks. The Sherman tank proved to be a crucial asset, its 75mm gun blasting the attackers and forcing them to keep their distance. But the SS were relentless, and they managed to knock out the tank with a Panzerfaust, a handheld anti-tank weapon. The crew survived, but the tank was disabled.

With the tank out of action, the defenders were forced to fall back to the castle walls. They fought with rifles, pistols, and even grenades, holding off the SS with desperate courage. At one point, the SS managed to breach the outer gate, but they were driven back by a counterattack led by Lee and Gangl. The fighting was intense and chaotic, with the castle's narrow corridors and stairways becoming a deadly maze. The prisoners, who had been given weapons, fought alongside their rescuers, their survival instincts overriding any fear. Jean Borotra, in particular, distinguished himself by running through enemy fire to deliver messages between the defenders.

As the afternoon wore on, the defenders' ammunition began to run low. They knew they couldn't hold out much longer. But then, just as the situation seemed hopeless, help arrived. A relief force from the 142nd Infantry Regiment, which had been alerted by a desperate message sent via a courier, finally reached the castle. The SS, seeing the American reinforcements, broke off the attack and retreated. The battle was over. The prisoners were safe, and the unlikely alliance had held.

Aftermath and Legacy

The Battle of Castle Itter was a tiny skirmish in the vast tapestry of World War II, but its significance far outweighs its size. It was the only time in the entire war that American and German forces fought together as allies. Major Josef Gangl, the German officer who had defied his own side, was killed by a sniper's bullet during the battle. He was posthumously honored by the Austrian resistance, and his sacrifice is remembered as a symbol of the moral courage that some individuals found even in the darkest of times. Captain Jack Lee survived the war and was awarded the Distinguished Service Cross for his actions. The French prisoners were all liberated and returned to France, where many of them resumed their political careers.

The story of Castle Itter is a reminder that history is not always black and white. In the chaos of war, alliances can shift, and enemies can become friends. It is a testament to the power of human decency and the instinct to protect the innocent, even when it means turning against your own side. The battle has been the subject of books, documentaries, and even a song by the band Sabaton, but it remains a relatively obscure footnote in the annals of World War II. Yet for those who know it, it stands as one of the most remarkable and inspiring episodes of the conflict.

Conclusion

The Battle of Castle Itter is a story that defies easy categorization. It is a war story, but it is also a story of reconciliation and shared humanity. In the final days of a war that had consumed millions of lives, a handful of men and women chose to see each other not as enemies, but as people. They fought together, bled together, and in some cases died together, all in the name of a cause that transcended national boundaries. As we look back on the history of World War II, it is easy to focus on the grand battles and the sweeping narratives of good versus evil. But it is in the small, improbable moments like the one at Castle Itter that we find the true measure of the human spirit. It is a story that deserves to be remembered, not just as a curiosity, but as a profound lesson in courage, loyalty, and the power of doing what is right, even when it seems impossible.
