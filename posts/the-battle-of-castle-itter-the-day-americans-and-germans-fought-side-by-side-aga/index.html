The Battle of Castle Itter: The Day Americans and Germans Fought Side by Side Against the SS

2026-07-19 · Modern History · Tags: The, historyuncovered, modern history

In the final, chaotic days of World War II, as the Third Reich crumbled and the map of Europe was being redrawn in blood and ink, an event unfolded in the Austrian Alps so improbable that it reads like the invention of a screenwriter with a taste for the absurd. On May 5, 1945—just two days before Germany's unconditional surrender—American soldiers and German Wehrmacht troops fought shoulder to shoulder against a fanatical SS assault. Their objective: to defend a medieval castle and the high-profile French prisoners held within. This was the Battle of Castle Itter, a conflict that defies every expectation of how the war in Europe ended, and one of the most remarkable, little-known stories of the entire conflict.

The Castle and Its Unlikely Prisoners

Castle Itter, a 13th-century fortress perched on a steep hill above the village of Itter in the Austrian Tyrol, had seen centuries of history—but nothing prepared it for the role it would play in the spring of 1945. After the Anschluss of 1938, the castle was seized by the Nazis, and by 1943 it had been converted into a prison for a very specific class of inmate: French VIPs deemed too valuable to kill outright but too dangerous to leave free.

The list of prisoners reads like a who's who of the French Third Republic. There was Paul Reynaud, the former Prime Minister who had led France during the German invasion of 1940. Édouard Daladier, another former Prime Minister and signatory of the Munich Agreement, was there too. So was General Maurice Gamelin, the commander-in-chief of French forces at the start of the war, whose failure to halt the German blitzkrieg had sealed his country's fate. Alongside them were Jean Borotra, a Wimbledon tennis champion turned Vichy official, and François de La Rocque, a right-wing political leader who had once been a hero of the far right before falling out of favor with the Nazis.

These men were not ordinary prisoners. They were symbols—living reminders of a France that had been humiliated, divided, and occupied. The Nazis kept them alive as bargaining chips, potential leverage in any negotiated peace. But as the Reich collapsed, their value was about to change dramatically.

The Collapse of the Reich and the SS Threat

By early May 1945, the German war machine was in its death throes. Hitler had shot himself in his Berlin bunker on April 30. Berlin had fallen to the Red Army. Field Marshal Wilhelm Keitel was preparing to sign the instruments of surrender. But in the Austrian Alps, where the mountains provided a natural redoubt, fanatical SS units remained active—and their orders were chilling.

The SS guards at Castle Itter, commanded by a brutal officer named Sebastian Wimmer, had received explicit instructions: if the castle could not be held, the prisoners were to be executed. No one was to be allowed to fall into Allied hands alive. Wimmer, a true believer in the Nazi cause, had every intention of carrying out those orders. The prisoners, well aware of their fate, knew that their only hope lay in escape or rescue—and time was running out.

On May 3, Wimmer and his SS guards abandoned the castle, fleeing into the mountains as American forces approached. But the prisoners knew this was no reprieve. A larger SS force, under the command of a fanatical officer, was reportedly massing nearby with orders to storm the castle and liquidate everyone inside. The prisoners were trapped between a retreating enemy and an advancing one—and the advancing one wanted them dead.

The Desperate Plea and the Unlikely Alliance

With the SS guards gone, the prisoners made a desperate decision. They dispatched a Czech cook named Andreas Krobot, who had worked at the castle, on a bicycle to find help. Krobot pedaled through the Austrian countryside, dodging SS patrols, until he reached the town of Wörgl, where he encountered an American reconnaissance unit led by Captain John “Jack” Lee of the 23rd Tank Battalion, 12th Armored Division.

Lee, a tough, pragmatic officer, understood the gravity of the situation. But he also knew his resources were limited—a single Sherman tank, nicknamed “Besotten Jenny,” and a handful of infantrymen. Storming a castle defended by SS troops was not a mission for a reconnaissance squad. Lee needed help, and he needed it fast.

In a twist that defies belief, Lee found that help in the most unexpected place: a German officer. Major Josef Gangl, a Wehrmacht officer who had secretly joined the Austrian resistance, was stationed in Wörgl with a small contingent of German soldiers. Gangl, like many Wehrmacht officers by this point, was disgusted with the Nazi regime and desperate to end the war with as little bloodshed as possible. When Lee approached him, Gangl didn't hesitate. He agreed to fight alongside the Americans—not against his own countrymen, but against the SS, which he viewed as a criminal organization that had hijacked Germany.

Gangl brought with him a handful of his most trusted men, including a young tank commander named Sergeant Rolf Wagenfeld, who had a Panther tank at his disposal. The irony was not lost on anyone: an American Sherman and a German Panther, side by side, rolling toward a castle to save French prisoners from the SS.

The Battle: A Desperate Defense

On the morning of May 5, 1945, the combined American-German force arrived at Castle Itter. They found the prisoners alive but terrified, huddled in the castle's great hall. The SS force, numbering around 150 men, was already approaching from the surrounding hills. Lee and Gangl quickly organized a defense.

The defenders were a motley crew: about 20 Americans, 10 Germans, and the prisoners themselves. Lee, in a moment of dark humor, handed rifles to the elderly French politicians and told them to fight. Jean Borotra, the tennis champion, reportedly quipped that he had never fired a gun in his life—but he took the weapon anyway. General Gamelin, who had commanded millions of men just five years earlier, found himself holding a rifle in a medieval castle, preparing to defend his own life.

The SS attack came in waves. They had mortars, machine guns, and overwhelming numbers. The defenders, though outnumbered, had the advantage of the castle's thick stone walls and the firepower of the two tanks. The Sherman and the Panther traded fire with the SS, their shells tearing into the attackers' positions. The battle raged for hours, with the SS repeatedly attempting to breach the castle's gates. At one point, the SS managed to knock out the Sherman, but the defenders held.

Then, in a moment of tragedy that would mark the battle's end, Major Gangl was struck by a sniper's bullet. He died instantly—the last German officer to be killed in combat in World War II. His sacrifice, and his decision to fight alongside the Americans, would not be forgotten.

The Aftermath and a Legacy Nearly Lost

Just as the defenders were running low on ammunition and hope, American reinforcements arrived. A column of tanks and infantry from the 142nd Infantry Regiment, 36th Division, had been dispatched after Lee's radio calls for help. The SS force, now facing a full American armored assault, broke and fled into the mountains. The battle was over.

The prisoners were evacuated to safety, and the unlikely alliance was celebrated—briefly. But the story of Castle Itter quickly faded into obscurity, overshadowed by the enormity of the war's end. The surrender of Germany, the liberation of concentration camps, and the beginning of the Cold War all pushed this small, strange battle out of the headlines. For decades, it was little more than a footnote in military history, a curiosity mentioned in passing by historians who stumbled upon it.

In recent years, however, the Battle of Castle Itter has been rediscovered. Books, documentaries, and articles have brought it back into the light, and for good reason. It is a story that challenges our understanding of World War II—a war often framed as a clear-cut struggle between good and evil, with lines drawn in stark black and white. Here, those lines blurred. A German officer chose to fight against his own country's fanatics. An American captain chose to trust a man in an enemy uniform. And together, they saved lives.

Why This Story Matters Today

The Battle of Castle Itter is more than just a fascinating historical curiosity. It is a reminder that even in the darkest moments of human history, individuals can rise above ideology, nationalism, and hatred to find common ground. Major Gangl was a German officer who had served the Nazi regime—yet he recognized that the SS represented something far worse, and he was willing to die to stop them. Captain Lee was an American soldier fighting a brutal war—yet he was willing to trust a German officer, to fight alongside him, and to honor that trust.

In an era when history is often simplified into narratives of absolute good versus absolute evil, the story of Castle Itter offers a more nuanced, more human perspective. It reminds us that the people who lived through these events were not cardboard cutouts but complex individuals making difficult choices under impossible circumstances. It also serves as a powerful lesson about the importance of pragmatism, courage, and the willingness to see beyond the uniform to the person wearing it.

Conclusion

The Battle of Castle Itter lasted only a few hours, but its echoes have resonated for decades. It is a story of unlikely heroes, of a tennis champion firing a rifle, of a German major giving his life to save French prisoners, of an American captain who refused to accept that the enemy was always the enemy. In the chaos of a collapsing empire, a small group of men—American, German, and French—came together to do what was right, not what was expected. Their story deserves to be remembered, not just as a footnote of World War II, but as a testament to the enduring power of humanity in the face of inhumanity. The next time you hear a story about the war, remember Castle Itter—and the day the lines of battle were drawn not between nations, but between those who chose to protect life and those who chose to destroy it.
