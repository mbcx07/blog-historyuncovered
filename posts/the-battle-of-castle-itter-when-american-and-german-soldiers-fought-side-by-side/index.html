The Battle of Castle Itter: When American and German Soldiers Fought Side by Side Against the SS

2026-06-23 · Wars · Tags: wars, historyuncovered, The

In the final days of World War II, as the Third Reich crumbled into rubble and chaos, an extraordinary alliance formed in the Austrian Alps—one that defied every expectation of the conflict. At a medieval castle overlooking the village of Itter, American soldiers, German Wehrmacht troops, and a handful of French political prisoners united against a common enemy: the fanatical SS. This was the Battle of Castle Itter, the only time in the war that Americans and Germans fought together as allies. It was a desperate, improbable stand that saved the lives of some of France's most prominent citizens and produced a story so surreal that historians still marvel at its audacity.

The Unlikely Setting: Castle Itter and Its Prisoners

Castle Itter, a 19th-century fortress perched on a hill near the village of Itter in Tyrol, Austria, had been converted into a prison in 1943 by the SS. Its location—remote, mountainous, and difficult to assault—made it an ideal holding facility for high-value prisoners. These were not spies or resistance fighters, but France's elite: former prime ministers, military commanders, and political figures who had fallen out of favor with the Vichy government or were considered threats by the Nazis. Among them were Édouard Daladier, the French premier who signed the Munich Agreement; Paul Reynaud, another former premier; Maxime Weygand, a celebrated general; and Maurice Gamelin, the commander of French forces in 1940. Also imprisoned were trade union leader Léon Jouhaux, right-wing politician François de La Rocque, and tennis star Jean Borotra, who had served as a Vichy sports minister.

Life in the castle was tense but bearable—guards were generally lenient, and the prisoners were allowed to exercise in the courtyard. But as the war ground toward its conclusion, the SS guards grew edgy. By early May 1945, with Hitler dead and Berlin captured, the Nazi hierarchy was collapsing. Orders from Berlin were contradictory, and the SS command in the region, led by the ruthless Heinrich Himmler, had issued a directive to execute all prisoners rather than let them fall into enemy hands. The castle's commandant, SS-Obersturmführer Sebastian Wimmer, faced a dilemma. He had no desire to be a war criminal, but he also feared the consequences of disobedience. In the first days of May, he made a fateful decision: he and most of his guards fled, leaving the prisoners under the watch of a few loyalists.

The Desperate Plea: German Officer's Defection

With the SS gone, the prisoners were free in a technical sense, but they were trapped. The surrounding area was still held by SS units, and the nearest Allied forces were miles away. They had no weapons, no radio, and no way to know who to trust. The castle's cook, a Czech named Andreas Krobot, volunteered to bicycle to the nearest town to seek help. He managed to reach the village of Wörgl, where he found a group of American soldiers from the 12th Armored Division, but they were under orders to hold their position and not advance. However, Krobot also encountered a German officer—Major Josef Gangl, a Wehrmacht officer who had defied the SS and was now leading a small group of anti-Nazi soldiers. Gangl, a decorated veteran of the Eastern Front, had been disgusted by Nazi atrocities and had joined the Austrian resistance. He agreed to help the prisoners, but he knew his force was too small.

Gangl contacted the American command, explaining the situation. The American officer in charge, Captain John C. "Jack" Lee Jr., was skeptical but intrigued. Lee, a seasoned infantry commander, had seen enough of war to know that the enemy could sometimes be an ally. He decided to take a small detachment—14 soldiers from the 23rd Tank Battalion, plus a Sherman tank named "Besotten Jenny"—and drive to the castle. Gangl, meanwhile, gathered his own group: about a dozen German soldiers who had renounced the Nazi cause, along with a few Austrian resistance fighters. The two groups met on the road and, after a tense exchange, agreed to cooperate. The mission was simple: reach Castle Itter, protect the prisoners, and hold out until reinforcements arrived.

The Reluctant Allies: Assembling the Coalition

When Captain Lee and his men arrived at the castle on May 4, 1945, they found the prisoners alive but terrified. The SS had left, but a new threat loomed: a large SS division, the 17th SS Panzergrenadier Division, was retreating through the area, and its commanders had learned of the prisoners' existence. They planned to storm the castle and execute everyone inside. Lee immediately organized a defense. He ordered the Sherman tank to be positioned at the main gate, but the tank's crew discovered that the castle's drawbridge was too narrow for the vehicle. They had to leave the tank outside, where it could provide covering fire but was vulnerable. Lee then distributed rifles and pistols to the prisoners, many of whom had never fired a weapon. The French generals, despite their age, insisted on joining the fight. Jean Borotra, the tennis star, volunteered to act as a runner, carrying messages between the castle and the outside world.

The coalition was bizarre: American GIs, German Wehrmacht soldiers, Austrian resistance fighters, and French dignitaries all sharing the same trenches. They spoke different languages, had different loyalties, but shared a common goal. Gangl, who spoke excellent French, served as a translator. He also used his knowledge of German military tactics to advise Lee on how to repel an SS assault. The prisoners, meanwhile, proved surprisingly capable. General Gamelin, who had been criticized for his passivity in 1940, took charge of the castle's defenses, positioning sandbags and setting up firing positions. Daladier, a man of action, manned a machine gun. The atmosphere was surreal, but the threat was real.

The Battle: Outnumbered and Outgunned

On the morning of May 5, the SS attacked. They came in force—about 100 to 150 soldiers, armed with mortars, machine guns, and Panzerfausts. The defenders had perhaps 30 men, plus the tank, but the tank was a sitting duck. The SS first knocked out the Sherman with a Panzerfaust, killing the tank commander. The crew escaped, but the tank was now a burning wreck. The SS then launched a series of infantry assaults, trying to breach the castle's walls. The defenders held, using the castle's thick stone walls for cover. The French prisoners, many of them veterans of World War I, fought with surprising ferocity. General Weygand, who was in his late 60s, fired a rifle from a window, picking off SS soldiers with cold precision. The battle raged for hours, with the SS unable to break through. They tried to use a captured Austrian resistance fighter as a human shield, but the defenders refused to surrender.

Meanwhile, Borotra, the tennis star, had managed to slip out of the castle and run through the woods to summon reinforcements. He found an American patrol from the 142nd Infantry Regiment, which had been advancing toward the area. The patrol, led by Lieutenant Harry Basse, quickly moved to the castle. Their arrival turned the tide. The SS, now facing a larger force, began to retreat. By the afternoon, the battle was over. The defenders had suffered only one casualty: Major Gangl, who was shot by an SS sniper while trying to protect the prisoners. He died instantly, but his sacrifice ensured the safety of the others. The SS forces withdrew, and the prisoners were evacuated to safety. The battle had lasted less than a day, but it had been fierce and deadly.

The Aftermath and Legacy

The Battle of Castle Itter was a footnote in the vast canvas of World War II, but it has become a legend among historians. It was the only time in the war that American and German soldiers fought side by side against a common enemy. The prisoners were liberated and returned to France, where they resumed their political careers. The American soldiers were awarded medals, but the story was largely forgotten until recent decades, when it was rediscovered by historians and popularized in books and documentaries. The castle itself still stands, a testament to the strange twists of war.

For those who study military history, the battle offers a profound lesson: in the chaos of conflict, alliances can shift, and enemies can become allies. The German soldiers who fought alongside the Americans were not traitors—they were men who had seen the evil of Nazism and chose to resist it. Their bravery, along with that of the French prisoners who took up arms, reminds us that courage and humanity can emerge even in the darkest of times. The Battle of Castle Itter is a story of unlikely heroes, a moment when the lines between friend and foe blurred, and a testament to the enduring power of the human spirit.

Conclusion

History is often written in broad strokes—nations, armies, and ideologies—but it is the small, improbable stories that truly capture the imagination. The Battle of Castle Itter is one such story: a handful of men, thrown together by fate, who defied the odds and saved lives. It challenges our assumptions about World War II, reminding us that not all Germans were Nazis, not all Frenchmen were collaborators, and that even in the midst of total war, decency could prevail. As we look back on this remarkable episode, we honor the memory of Major Josef Gangl, Captain John Lee, and all those who fought that day. Their alliance was a fleeting moment of sanity in a world gone mad—a reminder that even in the ruins of war, hope can take root.
