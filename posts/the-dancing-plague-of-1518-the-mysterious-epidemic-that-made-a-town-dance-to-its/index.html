The Dancing Plague of 1518: The Mysterious Epidemic That Made a Town Dance to Its Death

2026-07-19 · Mysteries · Tags: mysteries, The

In the sweltering summer of July 1518, a woman named Frau Troffea stepped out of her half-timbered house in the bustling city of Strasbourg and began to dance. It was not a joyful jig or a festive reel—it was a frantic, convulsive, and uncontrollable twitching that would not stop. Within a week, a dozen others had joined her, their limbs flailing as if possessed. By the end of the month, nearly 400 people were dancing in the streets, day and night, until some collapsed from exhaustion, heart attacks, or strokes. Dozens died. This was the Dancing Plague of 1518, one of history's most baffling and macabre mysteries—a mass phenomenon that defies simple explanation even five centuries later. Why did an entire town dance itself to death? The answer remains as elusive as the steps themselves.

The Outbreak: What Happened in Strasbourg?

The chronicles of Strasbourg, preserved in municipal records and the writings of contemporary physicians, paint a vivid and disturbing picture. It began on July 14, 1518, when Frau Troffea, a widow, began dancing in the narrow cobblestone street near the city's grain market. She danced for six straight days, her feet bleeding, her body drenched in sweat, yet she could not stop. Alarmed neighbors summoned a priest, who attempted to exorcise her, but to no avail. Within days, more people joined—first a handful, then dozens. By the end of the first week, the city authorities were in a panic.

Their response, by modern standards, seems almost criminally misguided. Believing that the dancers were suffering from an excess of "hot blood" or a form of divine punishment, the city council decided that the best cure was to let them dance it out. They hired professional musicians to play lively tunes, cleared a large wooden stage in the city square, and even brought in strong men to hold the dancers upright when they faltered. The theory was that if the dancers were encouraged to dance to exhaustion, the affliction would pass. Instead, it spread like wildfire. The music attracted more participants, and the stage became a writhing mass of humanity. Some dancers reportedly danced for weeks without rest, their bodies emaciated, their faces contorted in agony. By the time the plague subsided in September, over 400 people had been affected, and at least 30 had died—though some estimates put the death toll much higher.

Theories and Explanations: From Poison to Mass Hysteria

Historians and scientists have proposed a range of theories to explain the Dancing Plague, each with its own strengths and weaknesses. The most famous is ergotism, a condition caused by ingesting ergot fungus, which grows on damp rye and contains psychoactive compounds similar to LSD. Ergotism can cause hallucinations, muscle spasms, and a burning sensation in the limbs—symptoms that might be mistaken for dancing. However, ergotism typically causes convulsions and gangrene, not coordinated, rhythmic dancing. Moreover, the outbreak in Strasbourg was highly localized, and no other symptoms of ergot poisoning—such as vomiting or skin lesions—were recorded. The theory has largely been dismissed by modern scholars.

A more compelling explanation is mass psychogenic illness, also known as mass hysteria. This occurs when a group of people, often under extreme stress, develops physical symptoms with no organic cause. In 1518, Strasbourg was reeling from a series of crises: a severe famine, outbreaks of syphilis and other diseases, and deep religious anxiety about the end of the world. The region had also experienced a devastating flood the previous year. The psychological pressure on the population was immense. In such conditions, a single person's bizarre behavior can trigger a collective response, especially when authorities reinforce the behavior by providing music and a stage. The dancers may have been experiencing a dissociative state, a form of extreme stress reaction that manifested as involuntary movement.

Other theories include religious fervor—some scholars point to the cult of St. Vitus, the patron saint of dancers, whose feast day falls in June, and whose devotees were said to dance to honor him. But the Strasbourg dancers showed no signs of piety; they were described as "raving" and "frothing." There is also the possibility of a mass poisoning from a contaminated water supply, but no evidence supports this. The most likely explanation, according to contemporary psychologists, is a combination of extreme stress, social contagion, and the power of suggestion—a perfect storm of psychological vulnerability.

The Historical Context: Strasbourg in 1518

To understand the Dancing Plague, we must look at the world in which it occurred. Strasbourg, then a free imperial city of the Holy Roman Empire, was a prosperous trading hub on the Rhine River. But prosperity was fragile. The early 16th century was a time of profound upheaval: the Protestant Reformation was just beginning to stir, and the Catholic Church was rife with corruption and superstition. The common people lived in constant fear of famine, plague, and the wrath of God. The summer of 1518 was particularly harsh—a failed harvest had led to widespread hunger, and the city's poor were desperate. Chroniclers noted that the dancers were mostly from the lower classes, though some were artisans and even a few wealthy citizens.

Religious beliefs played a crucial role. Many believed that dancing was a punishment for sin, or that the dancers were possessed by demons. The city's physicians, who were consulted, declared that the affliction was a "natural disease" caused by "hot blood" and recommended the dance cure—a decision that likely exacerbated the crisis. The authorities' response, while well-intentioned, turned a small outbreak into a mass phenomenon. By providing a stage and musicians, they legitimized the behavior and made it socially acceptable to join in. This is a classic example of how cultural expectations can shape the expression of psychological distress.

Similar Outbreaks: The Dancing Manias of the Middle Ages

The Strasbourg event was not unique. Europe had seen several such outbreaks in the centuries before. In 1237, a group of children in Erfurt, Germany, danced uncontrollably until they collapsed, and some reportedly died. In 1374, a dancing plague swept through the Rhineland, affecting towns in Germany, France, and the Low Countries. That outbreak was even more widespread, with hundreds of people dancing in the streets, often in a trance-like state, and some reportedly tearing their clothes and screaming. The 1374 plague was also linked to St. Vitus, and many sufferers were taken to chapels dedicated to the saint for healing.

There were also smaller incidents in the 15th century, including one in 1463 in the town of Apulia, Italy, where a group of women danced until they were exhausted. The pattern was always similar: a single person begins dancing, others join, the behavior spreads, and authorities respond with a mix of fear and misguided attempts to cure. The fact that these outbreaks occurred repeatedly suggests that they were not random aberrations but rather a recurring phenomenon rooted in the psychological and social conditions of medieval Europe. The 1518 outbreak was the most severe and best-documented, but it was part of a broader pattern of mass psychogenic illness that has been observed in other contexts, such as the "dancing mania" of Madagascar in the 19th century and the "laughing epidemic" of Tanganyika in 1962.

Modern Interpretations: What Can We Learn?

Today, historians and psychologists view the Dancing Plague as a textbook case of mass psychogenic illness. The condition, also known as conversion disorder, occurs when psychological stress is converted into physical symptoms. In a tightly knit community with shared beliefs and high anxiety, a single person's behavior can trigger a cascade of similar responses in others. The key factors are:

  • Extreme stress or trauma (famine, disease, religious fear)
  • A trigger event (Frau Troffea's dance)
  • Social reinforcement (the authorities' decision to provide music and a stage)
  • Cultural beliefs that legitimize the behavior (the idea of St. Vitus's dance)

Modern research has shown that such outbreaks are not limited to the Middle Ages. In the 20th century, there were cases of "mass hysteria" in factories, schools, and even entire communities, where people reported symptoms like fainting, twitching, or laughing without any organic cause. The Dancing Plague reminds us that the human mind is profoundly susceptible to social influence, and that our bodies can manifest psychological distress in dramatic and unexpected ways. It also highlights the importance of understanding the cultural context in which such phenomena occur—what seems irrational to us may have been a logical response to a world filled with supernatural beliefs and existential dread.

The Enduring Mystery: Why Did They Dance?

Despite the advances in psychology, the Dancing Plague of 1518 still holds a stubborn core of mystery. The sheer scale and intensity of the outbreak—hundreds of people dancing for weeks, some to the point of death—seems to exceed what we would expect from simple stress or suggestion. Some researchers have proposed that the dancers may have been suffering from a form of epilepsy or a neurological disorder, but no evidence supports this. Others have suggested that the rye bread consumed in Strasbourg that summer might have contained a particularly potent strain of ergot, but the lack of other symptoms makes this unlikely.

What remains most puzzling is the fact that the dancers seemed unable to stop, even when they were exhausted, bleeding, and in pain. They reportedly danced with a kind of frenzy that was both terrifying and mesmerizing. Some chroniclers wrote that they "danced as if they were possessed by the devil," and that their faces were "pale and distorted." The fact that the outbreak ended only after the authorities banned all public dancing and sent the afflicted to a shrine in the Vosges mountains suggests that the power of suggestion was also the power of cure—once the social reinforcement was removed, the behavior ceased.

Perhaps the true answer lies in the intersection of biology, psychology, and culture. The human brain is wired to respond to rhythm and movement, and in a state of extreme stress, the boundaries between voluntary and involuntary action can blur. The dancers may have been caught in a feedback loop: the more they danced, the more their bodies released endorphins and adrenaline, which in turn fueled the compulsion to continue. The music, the crowd, and the attention all amplified the effect. In a sense, they were trapped in a dance of their own making, a dance that their minds had created as a way to cope with an unbearable reality.

Conclusion

The Dancing Plague of 1518 remains one of history's most bizarre and unsettling mysteries. It is a stark reminder that the human mind is a fragile and mysterious thing, capable of producing behaviors that defy rational explanation. While we can now identify the likely mechanisms—mass psychogenic illness, social contagion, and the power of belief—we still cannot fully explain why a whole town would dance itself to death. Perhaps we never will. But the story of Frau Troffea and her fellow dancers endures as a testament to the strange and often terrifying depths of human psychology. It challenges us to look beyond the surface of historical events and consider the complex interplay of mind, body, and society. And it leaves us with a haunting question: if such a thing could happen in 1518, could it happen again? The answer, as history shows, is yes—but the dance will always be a mystery.
