The Battle of Castle Itter: The Day Americans and Germans Fought Together Against the SS

2026-07-10 · Wars · Tags: wars, The, historyuncovered

In the final, chaotic days of World War II, as the Third Reich crumbled and the Allies closed in from all sides, an extraordinary event unfolded in the Austrian Alps—one that defied every expectation of the conflict. On May 5, 1945, a ragtag force of American soldiers, German Wehrmacht troops, a French resistance fighter, and a single Sherman tank joined together to defend a medieval castle against a fanatical SS battalion. Their mission: to protect a group of high-profile French prisoners who were about to be executed. This was the Battle of Castle Itter, a skirmish that lasted only a few hours but stands as one of the most bizarre and heartening episodes of the war—a moment when enemies became brothers in arms to save lives, not to take them.

The Castle and Its Unlikely Guests

Schloss Itter, a picturesque castle perched on a hill above the Austrian village of Itter, had a history far older than the war itself. Built in the 19th century, it was later converted into a hotel, but in 1943 the Nazi regime seized it and transformed it into a prison for high-value French prisoners. These were not ordinary captives; they were the men who had once led France—former prime ministers, military commanders, and political figures who had either opposed the Vichy regime or fallen out of favor with the Nazis. Among them were Paul Reynaud, the former French prime minister; Édouard Daladier, another former premier; Generals Maurice Gamelin and Maxime Weygand; and Marie-Joseph Paul Yves Roch Gilbert du Motier, Marquis de La Fayette’s descendant, François de La Rocque, a right-wing politician. Also held there were trade union leader Léon Jouhaux and tennis star Jean Borotra, who had served as a Vichy sports minister but later became a resistance courier.

These prisoners were not treated as ordinary inmates. They were given relative comfort—good food, wine, and even a library—but they were still prisoners, and as the war turned decisively against Germany, their fate became increasingly precarious. In early May 1945, with the Red Army approaching from the east and American forces pushing into Austria, the SS guards at Itter received orders from Berlin: execute the prisoners before the Allies could liberate them. The command was clear, but not all Germans were willing to obey.

A Desperate Plea for Help

On May 3, 1945, the SS guards abandoned the castle, leaving the prisoners alone but still in danger. The prisoners, aware that the SS might return with a death squad, decided to take matters into their own hands. They sent a message to the nearby town of Wörgl, where a German major named Josef Gangl had defied the Nazi regime and was working with Austrian resistance fighters to surrender the town to the Americans. Gangl, a decorated officer who had grown disillusioned with Hitler, received the plea and knew he had to act. He drove to the American lines and requested a meeting with Captain John C. “Jack” Lee, commander of a tank company in the 12th Armored Division.

Lee, a tough, battle-hardened officer, was initially skeptical. Why would a German major want to help? But Gangl explained the situation: French VIPs were about to be murdered by the SS, and he needed American firepower to save them. Lee, despite the risks, agreed to help. He took a single Sherman tank, named “Besotten Jenny,” and a small squad of fourteen men, and set off for Castle Itter. Along the way, they were joined by a French resistance fighter, a former Foreign Legionnaire named Jean de Lattre de Tassigny’s aide, but more importantly, they had the tank and the determination to make a stand.

The Unlikely Alliance Forms

When Lee and his men arrived at the castle on May 4, they found the prisoners in a state of anxious relief. The Americans were greeted with champagne and handshakes, but the celebration was short-lived. The next morning, May 5, a reconnaissance patrol reported that a large force of SS troops—estimated at around 100 to 150 men—was approaching the castle. The SS had not forgotten the prisoners; they were coming to carry out the execution order. Lee and Gangl quickly organized a defense. The castle’s thick walls provided some cover, but the defenders were vastly outnumbered. They had the Sherman tank, which they positioned at the main gate, and a motley collection of rifles, pistols, and a few machine guns. The prisoners themselves, including the elderly generals and politicians, were handed weapons and told to fight. Some, like tennis star Jean Borotra, proved surprisingly capable.

The alliance was surreal: American GIs, German Wehrmacht soldiers, Austrian resistance fighters, and French VIPs all stood shoulder to shoulder, united against a common enemy. Gangl, the German major, was in constant communication with the outside world, trying to call for reinforcements. He managed to reach a German anti-aircraft unit that was still loyal to him, and they agreed to send a few men. But the SS attack came first.

The Battle for the Castle

At around 4:00 PM on May 5, the SS opened fire. The defenders returned fire from the castle windows and the tank’s main gun. The Sherman, commanded by Sergeant William S. McRae, proved to be a formidable deterrent, but the SS were determined. They launched a series of assaults, trying to outflank the castle and get close enough to use panzerfausts (bazooka-like weapons) against the tank. The fighting was intense and chaotic. At one point, the tank was hit and disabled, but the crew continued to fire its machine guns. The prisoners, many of whom had never fired a weapon in their lives, fought with surprising grit. General Gamelin, in his seventies, was seen calmly reloading a rifle. Paul Reynaud, the former prime minister, took cover behind a wall and fired at the attackers.

As the battle raged, Gangl was shot by an SS sniper. He died instantly, becoming the only German casualty of the battle. His sacrifice was not in vain; his calls for help had reached the American 142nd Infantry Regiment, which was already on its way. Just as the defenders were running low on ammunition and the SS were preparing a final assault, the sound of approaching American reinforcements filled the air. The SS, realizing they were outnumbered and outgunned, retreated into the woods. The battle was over. The prisoners were safe.

The Aftermath and a Forgotten Legacy

The Battle of Castle Itter was over in less than an hour, but its impact was profound. The prisoners were liberated and eventually returned to France, where they resumed their political careers. The American soldiers were hailed as heroes, but the story of the German major who gave his life to save his enemies was largely forgotten. Josef Gangl was posthumously honored by the Austrian resistance, and a street in Wörgl is named after him. But in the broader narrative of World War II, this battle is a footnote—a strange anomaly that doesn’t fit the clean lines of Allied versus Axis.

Why is it so little known? Perhaps because it complicates the story. Here were German soldiers fighting against the SS, the very embodiment of Nazi evil. It reminds us that not all Germans were Nazis, and that even in the darkest times, humanity can prevail. The battle also highlights the chaos of the war’s end, when loyalties shifted and old enemies could become allies in a matter of hours. For the men who fought there, it was a moment of clarity: the real enemy was not a nationality, but the ideology that had caused so much suffering.

The Lesson of Castle Itter

Today, Castle Itter still stands, a quiet monument to an extraordinary event. Visitors can walk its halls and imagine the unlikely defenders—Americans, Germans, Frenchmen, and Austrians—standing together against a common threat. The battle teaches us that history is rarely black and white. It shows that courage and compassion can emerge from the most unexpected places, and that even in the midst of total war, there are moments of grace. For those who study war, the Battle of Castle Itter is a reminder that the human spirit is capable of transcending the boundaries of nationality and ideology. It is a story that deserves to be told, not just as a curiosity, but as a testament to what we can achieve when we choose to see each other as human beings first.

Conclusion

The Battle of Castle Itter may have been small in scale, but its significance is immense. It was a battle where the lines of friend and foe blurred, where a German major died to save French prisoners, and where American soldiers fought alongside their former enemies to uphold the values of decency and life. In the annals of military history, it stands as a unique and inspiring episode—a reminder that even in the darkest hours, the light of humanity can shine through. As we reflect on the horrors of World War II, let us not forget this strange, beautiful moment when the war stopped making sense, and people simply did what was right.
